## Three Jobs Hiding Behind One Title

“LLM specialist” is not a job; it is a neighborhood. Within it live at least three distinct professionals. The prompt and evaluation designer shapes model behavior through instructions, examples, and test sets. The retrieval engineer builds the plumbing that feeds a model accurate, current company knowledge. The fine-tuning specialist changes the model itself, adjusting weights so it behaves consistently in a narrow domain.

Salaries, interview loops, and success metrics differ for each. Treating them as interchangeable is the root of most failed hires, and a pass on one skill says very little about the others.

## Prompting Roles: Judgment Over Cleverness

In 2026, nobody is impressed by a clever one-liner. Models follow instructions well enough that the differentiator has moved to judgment: knowing what to ask, constraining the output, and proving a prompt holds up on messy real inputs.

Strong candidates talk about evaluation sets, regressions when a model version changes, and cost per thousand calls. Weak ones show a screenshot of a single impressive answer. If a candidate cannot explain how they would notice a prompt quietly degrading after a vendor update, they have used prompting, not practiced it.

## RAG Engineers: Where Production Pain Lives

Retrieval-augmented generation looks simple in demos and turns difficult at scale. The real work is unglamorous: chunking strategy for inconsistent documents, embedding choice, hybrid search, reranking, permission-aware retrieval, and freshness when source files change daily.

Candidates who have shipped RAG describe failures with specifics, such as a policy PDF that split mid-table and produced confident wrong answers. They measure retrieval quality separately from generation quality, because a fluent answer built on the wrong passage is the most expensive bug a business can have. Ask for a failure story. Polished success stories are cheap.

## Fine-Tuning Specialists: Rare and Often Unnecessary

Fine-tuning is the most glamorous request and the least frequently justified. Many teams reach for it when better retrieval or tighter prompts would solve the problem at a fraction of the cost. A credible specialist will say so.

They discuss when parameter-efficient methods such as LoRA make sense, how to curate and clean training data, how to avoid catastrophic forgetting, and how to evaluate against a held-out set. A candidate who recommends fine-tuning for every problem is selling a hammer. The best hires often talk you out of the project.

## What Employers Actually Test

Interview loops have moved from trivia to work samples. Across serious teams, three signals dominate:

- A live debugging task on a broken pipeline, where the reasoning matters more than the fix
- An evaluation exercise: define “good” for an ambiguous output and build a way to measure it
- A trade-off discussion covering latency, cost, privacy, and safety, including when not to use an LLM

Beyond skills, interviewers watch for honesty about limits. Hallucination risk, data leakage, prompt injection, and model drift are daily realities, and candidates who name them calmly tend to be the ones who have been burned before. Certificates and course badges rarely move the needle. A small, documented project with measurable results does.

## FAQs

### Q1. Is fine-tuning required for most LLM jobs?

No. Most business use cases are solved with strong prompting and retrieval first. Fine-tuning becomes worthwhile only when you need consistent tone, format, or domain behavior that those methods cannot deliver. Hire a specialist who can justify the choice with evaluation data rather than defaulting to the most expensive option.

### Q2. Do LLM specialists need to code?

Yes, in nearly every production role. Even prompt-focused specialists write evaluation scripts, call APIs, and read logs. RAG and fine-tuning roles require stronger Python, data-handling, and deployment skills. Non-coding prompt work exists, but it rarely covers the reliability and cost responsibilities employers now expect.

### Q3. Can a certificate prove LLM expertise?

Not on its own. Certificates show exposure to concepts, while employers want evidence of shipped work: a documented pipeline, measurable results, and honest notes on what failed. Pair any credential with a small project and the ability to explain trade-offs under questioning.

### Q4. Is four to six weeks realistic for hiring an LLM specialist?

Yes, with a clear role definition and prepared assessments. Delays usually come from vague requirements and slow feedback, not a lack of candidates. Verified shortlists and a fixed interview loop keep strong talent from accepting competing offers while you deliberate.

### Q5. Which role should a small company hire first?

Start with a RAG-minded engineer who also understands prompting. Most early value comes from connecting models to company data reliably. Fine-tuning can wait until you have usage data, evaluation sets, and a clear gap that simpler methods cannot close.
